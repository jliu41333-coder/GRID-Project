Great Ideas, Spring 2026

Does being seen change what you can do?

An exploration of how others' perceptions shape, or don't shape, what we're able to achieve.

James Liu and Sebastian Maher  /  Ms. Welin

The Question
What we set out to answer

From social media likes to prison surveillance, from Emerson's essays to Foucault's Panopticon, people have always wondered whether being watched matters. We explored whether the gaze of others is fuel, a cage, or just a distraction.

To what extent do others' perceptions of you impact your achievements?
The "yes" side
Others' eyes push us forward
Debord, Foucault, and Sartre argue we are shaped by external attention. We perform, conform, and achieve partly for whoever is watching.
The "no" side
Real achievement comes from within
Emerson, King, and Ryan and Deci argue that chasing approval is a trap. The most meaningful work happens when you stop listening to the crowd.
The Sources
Six perspectives on perception and achievement

Click any card to read more.

Society of the Spectacle
Guy Debord, 1967
Class source

Human life has shifted from being to appearing. We achieve for strangers' approval and mistake their validation for real fulfillment.

Debord argues that modern capitalism creates an environment where identity is defined by what we project, not who we are. People chase likes, followers, and prestige as a substitute for actual fulfillment. His work is the clearest "yes" argument in our project: not only do others' perceptions matter, we actively hunt for them. This is key for understanding how social media amplifies the power of the external gaze over individual achievement.
Self-Reliance
Ralph Waldo Emerson, 1841
Class source

"Society everywhere is in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members." True genius trusts itself first.

Emerson's essay argues that conforming to others' expectations destroys a person's authenticity and potential. The crowd will always pull you toward mediocrity. This is our clearest "no" voice: meaningful achievement has to come from internal conviction, not external approval. Emerson makes the case that surrendering to how others define success is a betrayal of the self, and produces work that is always weaker than what the self, unobserved, might create.
Letter from Birmingham Jail
Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., 1963
Class source

King broke unjust laws in the face of social pressure. Progress sometimes means ignoring what others think of you entirely.

King argues that moral conviction has to override the judgments of society, including moderates who urged patience and conformity. His achievements, and those of the civil rights movement, came precisely by resisting what dominant perception expected of Black Americans. This makes King a powerful "no" voice: the greatest achievements are often made in the face of hostile perception, not because of it. Success built on social approval is fragile, but success rooted in justice is durable.
Panopticism
Michel Foucault, 1975
Own research

When people believe they are being watched, they regulate themselves even without a real observer. The gaze gets internalized.

Foucault's theory of panopticism, drawn from Bentham's Panopticon prison design, shows that the belief in observation is as powerful as actual observation. People discipline their own behavior when they think someone is watching. Applied to our question, this explains why performance at work or school spikes around an audience. It is a nuanced "yes": others' perceptions do not just influence us from the outside, they become a permanent internal monitor we carry around.
Bad Faith and Self-Deception
Sartre, via Du Plessis, 2023
Own research

When we chase external validation, we lie to ourselves. "Bad faith" is what happens when we abandon our real values to earn approval.

Sartre's concept of mauvaise foi (bad faith) describes how humans deceive themselves to stay comfortable, often by conforming to social benchmarks they do not actually believe in. When people change their goals to win the approval of others, they are living in bad faith. This source connects both sides: yes, external perception shapes behavior, but at the cost of authenticity. Real achievement, for Sartre, can only come from radical freedom, choosing your own values rather than borrowing them from the crowd.
Intrinsic Motivation Theory
Ryan and Deci, via O'Hara, 2017
Own research

Psychology backs it up: people are more successful when driven by internal goals, not by trying to impress anyone.

Ryan and Deci's Self-Determination Theory gives the "no" camp some scientific grounding. Their research shows that intrinsically motivated people consistently outperform those chasing external rewards or social approval when it comes to creativity, persistence, and overall wellbeing. That said, the theory does not pretend external validation is meaningless. It accepts that it is common and sometimes motivating, but argues that internal drive is always a stronger and more lasting foundation for achievement.
Conclusions
What we found

After going through six perspectives across philosophy, psychology, history, and critical theory, we landed on a nuanced answer: perception matters, but how much depends on whose perception it is and what you are actually trying to do.

01
The gaze is real and it is powerful

Debord and Foucault show that external perception genuinely shapes behavior, often at a deep psychological level. Pretending it does not exist is naive.

02
But it can corrupt what we achieve

Sartre and Emerson both warn that chasing others' approval leads to bad faith. Work shaped for an audience is almost always weaker for it.

03
The biggest achievements often defy the crowd

King's letter is the clearest proof: transformative achievement often happens in opposition to prevailing perception, not because of it.

04
Science sides with internal motivation

Ryan and Deci's research confirms the philosophical intuition. Intrinsic motivation produces deeper, more lasting achievement than external validation does.
