Great Ideas, Spring 2026

Does being seen change what you can do?

Looking at how other people's opinions of us shape what we're able to accomplish, and when they don't matter at all.

James Liu and Sebastian Maher

The Question
What we set out to answer

In today's massively interconnected world, people are constantly under the influence of others. Whether it's the simple influence created by social media, or the pressure that is created by their friends and family, people face influence wherever they go. We decided to explored this problem through 6 thinkers across different fields to figure out whether other people help you, hold you back, or get in the way.

To what extent do others' perception of you impact your achievements?
The yes side
Others' eyes push us forward
Debord, Foucault, and Sartre argue we are shaped by outside attention. We perform, conform, and achieve partly for whoever is watching.
The no side
Real achievement comes from within
Emerson, King, and Ryan and Deci all make the case that chasing approval gets in the way. The work that lasts tends to come from people who stopped caring what others thought.
The Sources
Six takes on perception and achievement

Click any card to read more.

Society of the Spectacle
Guy Debord, 1967
Class source

Life has shifted from being to appearing. We end up chasing strangers' approval and calling it success.

Debord's argument is that modern capitalism turns identity into something you perform rather than something you have. People chase likes, followers, and status as a stand-in for actual fulfillment. For our project this is the strongest case that outside perception matters, because according to Debord we are not just influenced by it, we actively go looking for it. That dynamic is central to understanding why social media has made the external gaze feel so high-stakes.
Self-Reliance
Ralph Waldo Emerson, 1841
Class source

Society everywhere is in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members. Trusting yourself is the only way forward.

Emerson's whole essay is built around the idea that caring what others think is how you end up living someone else's life. The crowd pulls you toward average. His essay is probably the clearest no in our project: real achievement has to come from inside, and letting other people's definitions of success set your direction is a way of selling yourself short. Work done for an audience ends up being weaker than work done for yourself.
Letter from Birmingham Jail
Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., 1963
Class source

King broke unjust laws while most of society told him to wait. Sometimes progress means ignoring what others think entirely.

King's letter pushes back against the moderates who wanted him to slow down and work within what society found acceptable. The civil rights movement succeeded in part because King and others refused to let dominant perception set the limits of what was possible. That makes this one of the strongest no voices in the project. The biggest achievements in history often happened against prevailing perception, not because of it, and King's letter is probably the best example of that anywhere in the class sources.
Panopticism
Michel Foucault, 1975
Own research

People regulate themselves when they think they are being watched, even when nobody actually is.

Foucault takes Bentham's Panopticon prison and uses it to explain how surveillance works on a social level. You do not need an actual observer watching you. The possibility of being watched is enough to change how you behave. That applies directly to our question because it explains why people work differently around an audience than they do alone. The perception itself becomes internalized, turning into a voice inside your head that you carry everywhere, even when no one is actually looking.
Bad Faith and Self-Deception
Sartre, via Du Plessis, 2023
Own research

Chasing validation means lying to yourself about what you actually want. Sartre calls this living in bad faith.

Sartre's concept of mauvaise foi describes the way people deceive themselves to avoid the discomfort of real freedom. One common form of that is shaping your goals around what will earn approval from others. When you do that, you are not really pursuing what you want, you are pursuing a version of yourself that other people will validate. This source sits between both sides of our argument: yes, outside perception shapes what people do, but Sartre sees that as a failure of authenticity rather than a path to genuine achievement.
Intrinsic Motivation Theory
Ryan and Deci, via O'Hara, 2017
Own research

People perform better when they actually care about what they are doing, not when they are trying to impress someone.

Ryan and Deci's Self-Determination Theory is the most research-backed piece in our project. Their work shows that people who are internally motivated consistently outperform those driven by external rewards or approval, in creativity, persistence, and overall wellbeing. They are not saying external feedback is worthless. They just argue that it makes a weaker foundation. If the main reason you are doing something is to get recognition, you will tend to do less interesting work than someone who genuinely cares about the thing itself.
Conclusions
What we found

Going through these six sources, we kept landing in the same place: perception does matter, but the more you rely on it, the more it tends to work against you. Here is roughly how we broke it down.

01
The effect is real and it is powerful

Debord and Foucault both show that outside perception shapes behavior in ways that go pretty deep. Ignoring that entirely is not really an option.

02
But it tends to corrupt what you make

Sartre and Emerson both get at the same problem from different angles. Work made for an audience is usually weaker than work made for yourself.

03
The biggest achievements usually go against the crowd

King is the most obvious example, but it holds broadly. The work that ends up mattering most often happened in spite of what people thought, not because of it.

04
The research backs internal motivation

Ryan and Deci put numbers to what the philosophers were arguing. When your motivation comes from inside, the work is better and it lasts longer.
